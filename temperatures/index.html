Smoking temperature chart: safe temps and pull temps for every cut

Two different numbers matter at the pit, and mixing them up is where most weekend cooks go wrong. Here is both — the USDA safety floor, and the temperature you actually pull at.

Published by The Weekend Pit · Last verified 14 August 2026 · Every number below is checked against USDA guidance and cross-checked against at least two credible sources, all named at the bottom of this page.

The short answer

Poultry is safe at 165°F, ground meat at 160°F, and whole cuts of beef, pork and lamb at 145°F with a 3-minute rest. But tough cuts like brisket and pork butt are pulled far higher — 195–205°F — not for safety, but because that is where collagen melts into gelatin and the meat goes tender.

Safety is a floor you must clear. Tenderness is a target you cook toward. Ribs and brisket pass the safety line hours before they are ready to eat.

USDA safe minimum internal temperatures

These are food-safety floors, not doneness recommendations. Measure at the thickest part, away from bone.

USDA safe minimums
FoodMinimum internal temperature
All poultry (whole, parts, ground)165°F
Ground beef & pork160°F
Whole cuts — beef, pork, lamb145°F + 3-minute rest
Fish145°F

Pull temperatures for smoking, cut by cut

Pit temp is what you set the smoker to. Pull temp is the internal reading where the meat comes off. Both are cross-checked against AmazingRibs, SmokedBBQSource and ThermoWorks.

About tri-tip. The 130–140°F range is standard restaurant practice for medium-rare, and it is what most barbecue sources will tell you. It is also below the USDA's 145°F minimum for whole cuts of beef. We print both numbers rather than pick one for you: the risk on an intact whole muscle is low because bacteria live on the surface and the sear handles that, but the official guidance is 145°F and you should know you are choosing to depart from it.
About ribs. ThermoWorks says 180–195°F held for 30 minutes. AmazingRibs says around 203°F. SmokedBBQSource says 180–185°F. That is not an error in one of them — ribs vary enormously by style, cut and how much fat is in them, which is exactly why the bend test outlives every number. Pick up the rack with tongs a third of the way down: when the surface cracks and the rack bends to about a 90-degree droop, they are done.

Carryover: the meat keeps cooking after you pull it

Big cuts continue climbing roughly 5°F or more after they leave the heat, as the hot exterior equalizes inward. That matters most on the cuts with narrow windows — poultry and pork loin — where 5°F is the difference between juicy and chalky. It matters least on brisket and butt, where you are already deep into a wide range.

Some professional cooks ride carryover deliberately, pulling chicken breast at 158°F and letting it drift up to safe. We print 165°F, because a number that reads as "pull poultry under temp" is a number that gets copied without the context around it.

Common questions

What temperature is brisket done at?

Pull brisket between 195°F and 205°F internal, with about 203°F as the usual sweet spot. Temperature is a guide, not the verdict — the real test is probe-tender, when a thermometer probe slides into the flat with no resistance. Collagen needs both heat and time to melt into gelatin, so two briskets can finish at different numbers.

Why did my brisket stop at 150°F?

That is the stall, and nothing is wrong. Around 150°F, moisture pushed to the surface of the meat evaporates fast enough that the cooling exactly cancels the heat of the pit — the same physics that keeps you from overheating on a July run. Physicist Greg Blonder tested this directly; the older explanations about melting fat or dissolving collagen turned out to be wrong. It can last hours. Wait it out, or wrap to cut the evaporation.

Is pork still safe if it is pink at 145°F?

Yes. The USDA lowered the whole-cut pork minimum to 145°F plus a 3-minute rest, and pork cooked to that temperature is frequently pink in the center. The colour comes from myoglobin, not from doneness. The old 160°F habit is why a generation of pork chops tasted like a wallet.

What temperature should I smoke chicken at?

Higher than you smoke everything else — 275–350°F. At the classic 225–250°F, the fat under the skin never renders and moisture stays trapped, which is exactly how you get rubbery skin. Skin needs real heat, not more time. For a spatchcocked bird, 300°F for roughly an hour and a half on a 4–5 lb chicken works well.

What is the difference between safe temperature and pull temperature?

Safe temperature is the point where the meat is no longer a food-safety risk — a floor set by the USDA. Pull temperature is where the meat tastes best, which for tough, collagen-heavy cuts is 40 to 60 degrees higher. A pork butt is safe long before it is good. A pork chop is safe and good at nearly the same moment. That gap is the whole craft of barbecue.

Do I need an instant-read thermometer?

Yes, more than you need almost any other piece of gear. Every number on this page is meaningless without a way to read it, and the dial thermometer built into a smoker lid is usually measuring air several inches from where the meat actually is, often off by 25°F or more. A leave-in probe for the meat and a fast instant-read for spot checks is the setup most people end up at.
